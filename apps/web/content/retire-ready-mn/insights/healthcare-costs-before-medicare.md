Retiring before Medicare can create one of the largest bridge costs in a retirement income plan. A household may feel ready based on savings and Social Security estimates, then discover that health insurance premiums, deductibles, and out-of-pocket costs materially change the monthly budget before age 65.

The first step is to separate the pre-Medicare years from the rest of retirement. A person retiring at 62 may need three years of private coverage, marketplace coverage, COBRA, spouse coverage, or another arrangement. The cost can be very different from the Medicare years that follow.

Income planning affects healthcare planning. Marketplace subsidies, when available, can depend on household income. IRA withdrawals, Roth conversions, capital gains, and part-time work may affect eligibility or premium costs. This creates a coordination problem. A withdrawal strategy that looks good for taxes may be less attractive after healthcare costs are included.

Cash reserves can help. If a retiree needs to manage taxable income carefully for coverage reasons, having after-tax cash available may reduce the need for large taxable withdrawals. Roth assets may also provide flexibility, depending on the household's situation.

Healthcare inflation should be included. Even after Medicare begins, retirees may face premiums, supplemental coverage, prescription drug costs, dental costs, vision costs, and expenses not fully covered. Long-term care is a separate planning concern and should not be treated as a normal medical bill.

Questions to answer before retiring before 65 include:

- What coverage will be used until Medicare?
- What are the premiums and deductibles?
- How will taxable income affect coverage cost?
- Which accounts will fund healthcare expenses?
- What changes when Medicare begins?
- Is there a plan for long-term care risk?

Healthcare costs do not automatically prevent early retirement. They do need to be modeled directly. A retirement income score is more useful when the healthcare bridge is not left as a vague estimate.

The bridge years can be especially important for Minnesotans who leave work at 60, 62, or 64. Those years may look affordable when the household only compares portfolio withdrawals with desired spending. The picture can change once premiums, deductibles, prescriptions, dental care, vision care, and possible out-of-network costs are added. Even one or two uncovered years can materially change the retirement cash reserve.

Income planning and health coverage planning are connected. Marketplace premiums and subsidies can be affected by taxable income. Roth conversions, capital gains, consulting income, pension income, and IRA withdrawals may all change the numbers. A strategy that looks efficient from an investment perspective can become less attractive if it raises healthcare costs during the bridge period.

Couples should avoid assuming both spouses reach Medicare at the same time. A common situation is one spouse retiring while the other still needs several years of coverage. Another is one spouse qualifying for Medicare while the younger spouse needs marketplace coverage or COBRA. The household income plan should show both birthdays, both coverage dates, and the cash flow needed until both are covered.

An HSA can be helpful if the household already has one, but it should not be treated as a complete answer. HSA funds may cover qualified medical expenses, yet the account balance may need to last decades. Using it too quickly before Medicare can leave fewer tax-advantaged dollars for later healthcare expenses. The plan should decide when HSA money is used and when it is preserved.

The most useful early-retirement healthcare review produces a year-by-year bridge. It names the coverage source, estimated premium, expected out-of-pocket exposure, account used to pay costs, and the tax impact of the income needed. Once those details are visible, the household can decide whether to retire earlier, work part time, delay retirement, reduce spending, or keep more cash available.

It is also worth planning for the administrative side. Employer coverage may end at a specific date, COBRA has election windows, marketplace coverage has enrollment rules, and Medicare enrollment has its own deadlines. A missed date can create unnecessary cost or coverage stress. The income plan should include those dates beside the financial estimates so the transition is not left to memory.

A conservative healthcare bridge includes a margin for surprises. Premiums can change, prescriptions can change, and a household that has rarely used medical care may still face a high-cost year. That does not mean early retirement is off the table. It means the plan should avoid using every available dollar for routine spending. A stronger retirement decision leaves room for healthcare uncertainty, especially in the years before Medicare begins.

The final check is emotional as much as mathematical. Some households are comfortable accepting more uncertainty for the freedom of retiring earlier. Others prefer to work longer so healthcare coverage and cash reserves feel stronger. Neither answer is universal. The best answer is the one the household can afford and live with calmly.

That confidence is part of the plan too.
