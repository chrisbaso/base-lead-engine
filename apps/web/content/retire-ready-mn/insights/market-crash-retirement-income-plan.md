A market crash affects retirees differently than accumulators. A worker who is still saving may be able to buy at lower prices and wait. A retiree who is withdrawing from the portfolio may have to sell investments while values are down. That is the core tension in retirement income planning.

The first question is whether essential spending depends on market withdrawals. If Social Security, pensions, and other dependable income cover the basics, a market decline is uncomfortable but less likely to force bad decisions. If the portfolio funds most essentials, the plan needs stronger guardrails.

The second question is how much liquidity exists outside stocks. Cash reserves, short-term bonds, and other lower-volatility assets can provide spending money during a downturn. Too much cash can reduce long-term growth. Too little can force selling at the wrong time. The balance should match the household's income need and risk tolerance.

The third question is whether withdrawals can adjust. Some expenses are fixed, such as housing, utilities, insurance, and food. Others may be flexible, such as travel, gifts, large purchases, or discretionary home projects. A written spending hierarchy can help the household know what changes first.

The fourth question is taxes. Down markets can create planning opportunities, including tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts or Roth conversions when account values are lower. But those moves must be coordinated with income needs and Medicare thresholds.

A crash plan may include:

- A spending reserve for near-term withdrawals
- A rule for reducing discretionary withdrawals
- A list of accounts to use first in stress
- A dependable income floor for essential expenses
- A rebalancing policy
- A tax review after major market moves

No plan makes a market crash pleasant. The goal is to avoid turning a temporary decline into a permanent retirement income problem. The households that handle volatility best usually made decisions before headlines got loud.
