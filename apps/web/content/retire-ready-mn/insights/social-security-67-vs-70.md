The difference between claiming Social Security at 67 and 70 is easy to describe and harder to evaluate. Waiting increases the monthly benefit through delayed retirement credits. That larger check can last for life and may become the survivor benefit for a spouse. But waiting also means three years without that income, so the bridge strategy matters.

For a Minnesota retiree with strong savings, the delay can be a way to buy more dependable income without buying a product. A larger Social Security check reduces the amount the portfolio must produce every month. That may allow a more flexible withdrawal strategy and can make market downturns easier to manage.

For a retiree with limited savings, waiting may still be attractive, but it can be harder to fund. If delaying forces large taxable IRA withdrawals or creates stress in the first retirement years, the benefit increase needs to be weighed against the cost of the bridge. The math is personal because the funding source matters.

Taxes can shift the answer. A person delaying benefits may have lower taxable income before Social Security begins. Those years can sometimes be used for Roth conversions, capital gain harvesting, or strategic IRA withdrawals. But those moves must be modeled carefully. A conversion that looks attractive in isolation can affect Medicare premiums or future tax brackets.

Health and family longevity also matter. Someone with a shorter life expectancy may prefer earlier benefits. Someone with a longer life expectancy, or a spouse who may depend on the survivor benefit, may value the larger age-70 check. This is not a prediction exercise. It is a risk management exercise.

The practical comparison should include:

- Monthly household income from 67 to 70
- Portfolio withdrawals needed during the bridge years
- Estimated taxes under both claiming ages
- Survivor income if either spouse dies first
- Medicare premium thresholds
- Required minimum distribution timing

Many households do not need a perfect answer. They need to avoid making the decision in isolation. Social Security timing should sit inside the larger retirement income plan, beside taxes, cash reserves, and portfolio risk.
