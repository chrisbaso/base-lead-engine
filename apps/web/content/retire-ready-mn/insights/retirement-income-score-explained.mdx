A retirement income score is a simple way to turn a complicated question into a useful first signal. The question is not just how much money is in the account. The question is how much monthly income that money can reasonably support when combined with Social Security and other dependable income sources.

RetireReadyMN uses a simplified baseline. It estimates portfolio income using a 4% withdrawal benchmark and adds your estimated monthly Social Security benefit. That combined number is compared with the monthly retirement income you said you want in today's dollars. The score is then grouped into Red, Yellow, or Green.

Green generally means the baseline income appears close to or above the desired income. It does not mean the plan is finished. Taxes, inflation, healthcare costs, sequence risk, and longevity can still change the answer. A Green score means the starting math is encouraging.

Yellow generally means the baseline shows a manageable gap. The gap may improve through Social Security timing, retirement age changes, savings changes, tax strategy, or a more detailed income plan. Yellow is often the most useful result because it shows where planning can make a measurable difference.

Red generally means the baseline income is meaningfully below the desired income. That does not mean retirement is impossible. It means the plan needs more detail. Spending goals, work timeline, guaranteed income, investment risk, housing decisions, and tax planning may all need to be reviewed together.

The score is intentionally educational. It does not recommend investments, annuities, insurance, or any specific product. It also does not promise that a withdrawal rate will work in every market. A real plan would look at account types, pension income, debt, cash reserves, health insurance, household taxes, and what happens if one spouse outlives the other.

The value of the score is direction. If the number is strong, you know what to protect. If the number is weak, you know what to investigate. If it is in the middle, you know which tradeoffs deserve attention before retirement decisions become permanent.

The score is most useful when it starts a better conversation. A household with a Red score may not need a dramatic change immediately. It may need to test a later retirement date, a lower spending target, different Social Security timing, or a clearer healthcare budget. A household with a Yellow score may need to focus on the few variables that move the result most. A household with a Green score may need to stress test the plan instead of assuming the work is finished.

For Minnesotans, the stress test should reflect local realities. Winter costs, property taxes, cabin ownership, rural healthcare access, seasonal travel, charitable commitments, and family support can all affect the monthly number. A national rule of thumb may miss those details. The income score gives a starting baseline, but the next step is to replace assumptions with household-specific numbers.

The score also does not decide how money should be invested. Two households with the same score may need different portfolios because their pensions, Social Security benefits, taxes, health needs, and emotional tolerance for market swings are different. The score compares income capacity with income goals. It does not recommend securities, insurance, annuities, or any specific account allocation.

The most helpful follow-up is a gap review. If projected income is below desired income, the review should identify the size of the gap, when the gap appears, and whether it affects essential spending or discretionary spending. A small discretionary gap may be easy to manage. A large essential gap requires more careful planning. The same score can mean different things depending on which expenses are at risk.

Think of the score as a dashboard light, not a diagnosis. It tells you where to look. The real planning work is deciding which levers are realistic, which tradeoffs are acceptable, and which risks deserve protection before retirement begins.

The most important input is desired monthly income. If that number is too low, the score may look better than real life will feel. If it is too high, the score may look worse than the household actually needs. A useful estimate separates fixed expenses from lifestyle spending and includes taxes, healthcare, home maintenance, insurance, and seasonal costs. Minnesotans who own a cabin, travel during winter, or support family should include those details rather than relying on a generic replacement ratio.

The score should be revisited when major decisions change. Retiring a year later, delaying Social Security, downsizing a home, paying off debt, converting IRA dollars, or changing the spending target can all move the result. That is why the checkup is designed as a first step. It gives the household a private starting point and helps identify which planning conversation is most likely to matter next.

Used that way, the score can reduce noise. It gives Minnesotans a clearer way to ask whether their income plan is aligned with the life they want to fund.
